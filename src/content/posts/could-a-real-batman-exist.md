Two.

That is how many street crimes a real Batman stops in a year in my city, swinging his fists. By my math, with real numbers. Then I worked out that the fists were never the point, and the real answer is stranger, and a lot sadder. Let me show my work.

Not the cape and the brooding. The job. One regular person, some gear, some training, some nerve, out at night stopping crime. Could it actually work? Not in a comic. In a body. So I picked a city, Richmond, Virginia, and tried to kill the fantasy with arithmetic.

## First, the honest part

I wanted real crime data the way I pulled real lap times for the F1 piece. Richmond does not hand it over. The open-data portal publishes traffic accidents, not crimes. The police crime app is a search box with no download. So I am not going to pretend I scraped a database I could not get. I built the model on the numbers Richmond does publish, every one of them sourced, and I label what I measured and what I assumed.

The real ones, 2024: 216 robberies of a person, 589 aggravated assaults. Call it 805 street crimes a year that a guy on a rooftop could even theoretically interrupt. About two a day, across sixty square miles. Now make him a physics problem.

## Mode one: a guy on patrol

Batman patrols. One body, moving, hoping to be close enough to a crime to stop it. So the question is geometry. How much of the city can he reach in time?

A mugging lasts a couple of minutes. Give him two. At a generous fifteen miles an hour through real streets, that buys half a mile of reach. That half-mile circle is his whole world. Everything outside it happens without him.

Half a mile of reach. Sixty square miles of city. Draw it to scale and the hero disappears.

<img class="chart-img" src="/images/charts/badassman-reach-thimble.svg" alt="Scale map of Richmond with a tiny half-mile interception diamond around one patrolling vigilante; the reach covers only a few percent of the city." loading="lazy" />

Run the numbers and a patrolling Batman intercepts about two crimes a year. Two. He is a thimble bailing the ocean, and the ocean is not even rough. The thing that stops him is not strength or speed or courage. It is that one man can be in one place, and one place is almost never where the crime is.

## "But nobody patrols at random"

Here is where you yell at me, and you are right. Batman is not supposed to be lucky. He is supposed to be smart. So I gave the model a brain, and it turns out there are exactly two ways to be smart, and criminologists have measured both.

**Target the right places.** Crime is not spread evenly, it clumps, hard, the same corners year after year. Park a credible presence on the worst one and crime there drops about twenty-four percent. That is the firmest number in policing, sixty-five studies deep. On a corner that would have seen fifty to two hundred crimes a year, that is twelve to forty-eight prevented. The catch: a city is thousands of corners and he is one body. He can stand on a handful.

**Target the right people.** A tiny number of offenders commit most of the crime. Wolfgang's old Philadelphia study: six percent of the kids did fifty-two percent of the offenses and eighty-two percent of the robberies. Take the vital few off the board and the leverage is enormous, on the order of thirty crimes a year per handful removed. The catch is bigger: "remove" means arrest, warrants, prosecution, follow-through. That is not a man in a mask. That is an institution. It already has a name, focused deterrence, and Boston ran it in the nineties and cut youth homicide sixty-three percent. None of it fits in a utility belt.

So the smart moves are real, and neither is a vigilante's to make. One man cannot cover the corners, and the part that works on people is a courthouse, not a cape.

## And money was never the problem

Here is the thing I had wrong for a while. I had priced this around Bruce Wayne, the famous six-hundred-and-eighty-two-million-dollar cost of being Batman. But that number is a cave, and a real person is not building a cave.

Strip the billionaire out. A concealable vest is a hundred and sixty dollars. A real kit, vest, light, trauma gear, a camera, a radio, runs about sixteen hundred, one time. The biggest recurring cost is not equipment, it is the gym membership to stay able to fight, about two grand a year. And one statistical life, the number every safety agency runs on, is worth about thirteen million dollars.

So if he saves even a fraction of one life, the gear pays for itself a thousand times over. On dollars, this is one of the best deals imaginable. Which means money was never the constraint, and never will be. The real cost is going to be measured in something he cannot buy down. To find it, you have to make him do the one thing the fantasy is actually about. You have to make him fight.

## The fists lose money

So I priced the fight itself. Every time Badassman throws a punch, he loses.

A trained amateur who physically jumps into a street crime in progress nets about negative fifty thousand dollars an intervention, once you count what happens to him. The half of the time he gets hurt even winning. The knife he was never going to disarm clean. The small, real chance he goes home in a bag. The one-in-ten odds that winning lands him in a courtroom next to the guy he stopped.

And it gets worse, because of how the fights sort. The ones he can actually win are single, unarmed, opportunistic, and they are too small to matter. The ones that matter arrive with a gun (three out of four aggravated assaults), a knife, or three guys, and those he loses. His skill and the stakes point in opposite directions. He can only win the fights that do not count, and the fights that count are the ones that hurt, kill, or indict him.

The fists are the worst-justified thing in the entire kit. Which is a problem, because the fists are the entire fantasy.

So either a real Batman is impossible, or I was measuring the wrong thing.

## I was measuring the wrong thing

Batman was never actually a brawler. Read the comics again. "Criminals are a superstitious, cowardly lot." The man dresses like a bat and works in the dark on purpose. The cowl is not armor. It is a billboard. The weapon was always fear, and I had spent the whole piece timing his forty-yard dash.

Here is what fear does that fists cannot. It scales.

One sniper does not clear a valley by hitting every soldier in it. He hits one, and a thousand men spend the next week crawling, because any of them could be next and none of them knows where he is. He occupies a hilltop the size of a beach towel and denies a square mile. Area covered, tiny. Area denied, enormous. That ratio is the only thing in this whole investigation that beats the geometry, because it does not care that he is one body. It only cares that he could, in principle, be anywhere.

That is the real Batman. Not a man who stops crimes. A rumor that stops them for him.

<img class="chart-img" src="/images/charts/badassman-two-channels.svg" alt="Log-scale bar chart: fists stop about two crimes a year, fear deters twelve to forty-eight at the measured floor, and an analogy ceiling of hundreds to thousands." loading="lazy" />

And it is measurable. Stopping crimes with your hands tops out around two a year. Deterring them runs on different physics, the same twenty-four percent on a hot corner, twelve to forty-eight prevented, by a guy who stopped no one. Six to twenty-four times the fists, and he just had to be a maybe. The cleanest experiment we have, randomized fifteen-minute patrols in the London Underground, found trouble dropped about a fifth and ninety-seven percent of the drop happened while the police were not there. The fear kept working after the body left. How big does the maybe get? Honestly I do not know, and anyone who hands you a hard number is selling something. The floor is real. The ceiling is a campfire story, and I am telling you up front it is a campfire story.

## A sniper who never fires is just a guy on a hill

Here is what the fantasy leaves out, and what the math will not let me.

Fear is not a thing you buy once. It is a thing you rent, by the week, in blood.

<img class="chart-img" src="/images/charts/badassman-decay-sawtooth.svg" alt="A sawtooth chart: deterrence spikes with each visible act then decays; frequent validation stays above the bite threshold, sparse validation falls below it." loading="lazy" />

A reputation decays. Leave a hot corner and the quiet lingers maybe thirty minutes, then the corner remembers it is a corner. A pure rumor, a threat nobody has watched act, fades toward nothing in a week or two. The decoy cop car works right up until everyone clocks that nobody is in it. So the legend has a half-life, and the only way to recharge it is to do the one thing that loses money and risks the bag: show up, be seen, and act, for real, on something dangerous. A sniper who never fires is just a man standing on a hill.

So the cheap, scalable, geometry-beating weapon runs entirely on the expensive, dangerous, geometry-bound one. The fear is the engine. The brawls are the fuel. And the fuel is him.

## The legend is immortal. The man is not.

Put the two together and the whole thing finally resolves, and not in the direction I wanted.

The deterrent does not age. The fear of Batman is worth the same twelve to forty-eight crimes a year in 1939 and in 2026, no matter who is breathing inside the suit. Flat line. Immortal.

The man holding it up is not.

<img class="chart-img" src="/images/charts/badassman-legend-vs-man.svg" alt="Survival curves: the probability the hero is still alive craters over the years while the deterrent he provides stays flat." loading="lazy" />

Keep the legend credible at a real street cadence, a genuine, witnessed, dangerous act every couple of weeks, and run each one through the fight's odds. The credible version of this hero is a coin flip to be dead inside eleven years. Get famous, get tested more, and it is five. Play it safe enough to live, and the legend slips below the line where anyone is scared, and you are back to a guy in a costume stopping two crimes a year and losing money on each.

That is the answer. The whole question, answered.

A real Batman can exist. The Batman part, the fear, the cowl, the rumor that empties a corner, is real, and it is the only thing in this entire build that does more than a rounding error. But the man cannot survive being him, because the only way to keep the rumor true is to keep walking into the fights that kill him. The geometry that doomed the fists never went away. It moved. It stopped being "how many crimes can one body stop" and became "how long does one body last keeping the legend scary." Criminals are a superstitious, cowardly lot. And that is exactly why Batman has to keep risking his life to stay frightening to them. The cowl outlives the guy in it. That is not tragic backstory. That is the load-bearing math.

Which is the part I kept not wanting to type, because in this whole stupid, beautiful experiment, the guy in the suit was supposed to be me. A 38-year-old with a vest, a gym membership, and a rumor he cannot afford to stop feeding.

So, no. I am not going to be Batman.

But I think I finally understand why the ones in the comics never take the cowl off, never seem to age, and always, always look so tired.

Next: there is exactly one job left where the math says put the suit on anyway. It is not crime.
